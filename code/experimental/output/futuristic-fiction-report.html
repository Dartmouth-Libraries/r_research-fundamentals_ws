The future got closer. Novelists had nothing to do with it.

Across works of futuristic fiction, the typical imagined future has moved from years ahead in the 1960s to years ahead in the 2020s. But the contraction belongs almost entirely to film and video games. Literary science fiction has not budged in seventy years.

The finding

Every work of futuristic fiction hides a number

It is the distance between the year a work was made and the year it depicts. 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) looks 33 years ahead. Blade Runner (1982) looks 37 years ahead. H. G. Wells's The Time Machine (1895) looks 802,701 years ahead. Call it the horizon.

Pooled across all media, the horizon has been shrinking by a year — a finding that looks clean until you ask what else changed over the same period. The answer is: almost everything. In the 1950s, of futuristic works were prose fiction. By the 2020s that was , while film had risen from to and video games had appeared from nothing.

Because film and games look nearer ahead than novels do, a shrinking pooled average could mean either that imaginations contracted or merely that the census changed. Separating the two requires looking inside each medium — which is what the graphic below lets you do yourself.

Five media, five answers

Fit a trend to each medium separately and the story falls apart

Each panel below fits its own straight line through the logarithm of the horizon against release year, then converts the slope into a percentage change per year. A medium counts as contracting only if its 95% confidence interval stays entirely below zero — a stricter and more honest bar than a bare p-value.

Who is actually pulling the future closer

Median horizon by release decade, log scale, shared across all five panels so the heights are comparable. Point size shows how many works sit behind each median.

Film and video games contract robustly. Prose fiction, television and comics show no trend that survives their own uncertainty — and comics rests on only 72 works across the whole period.

What this does not say

Release year explains under of the variation in horizon even with medium included. The trend is real, and it is small. Overwhelmingly, how far ahead a work looks is a choice made by one author for one story — not a property of the decade they worked in. A chart of medians can make a weak tendency look like a law, and this one would too if we let it.

The confound

What changed was who was doing the dreaming

If no single medium is contracting much, why does the pooled figure move at all? Because the population of futuristic fiction was replaced. The genre began this period as a literary form and ended it as a screen form, and the screen has always looked nearer ahead.

Controlling for medium cuts the pooled trend from to per year. Roughly of the decline everyone would have reported was this substitution, not a change in anybody's imagination.

How this was made

The choices behind the charts

Every figure on this page was computed in R and injected into this file by a script; none was typed by hand. The decisions that shaped them are listed below, including the ones that could reasonably have gone the other way.

What counts as the horizon
The imagined year minus the release year. The source file ships its own version of this column; we recomputed it independently and the two agree on all works with a recorded year.
Rows removed
works have no imagined year and cannot have a horizon. were released before , where a decade holds too few works for a stable median. belong to media with fewer than 50 works in total — a theme-park ride, an illustration, some radio and tabletop games. That leaves works across .
Rows deliberately kept
Nineteen works have horizons above a million years, topping out at 100 trillion. It would be easy to call these typos and delete them. They are not typos: Doctor Who's “Utopia” really is set at the end of the universe, and Stapledon's Last and First Men really does run ten million years out. Deleting them would have meant deleting the corpus's most ambitious imaginations — precisely the works that speak loudest to the question being asked.
Medians, not averages
With a variable whose maximum is 100 trillion, an average is meaningless: a single Doctor Who episode would drag any decade's mean into the billions. Medians and log scales absorb the tail without discarding it.
Known limitations
242 works are set across multiple years, so their single recorded year is a judgement call made by whoever built the dataset — some of the source values are genuinely vague (“7th millennium or beyond”). The corpus is Wikipedia- and Wikidata-derived, so it almost certainly documents recent English-language screen media better than older or non-anglophone print; the rising counts per decade are partly rising documentation rather than rising output. And the 2020s cover five years, not ten.
